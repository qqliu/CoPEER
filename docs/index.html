Co-PEER: Auditing Mathematics with Proofs of Error

Mathematical criticism you can check. Co-PEER connects a challenged claim to a concrete counterexample, a Lean-checked proof, and a review of whether the objection fits the source. An objection to a mathematical proof requires evidence of its own: a critic can misread a claim, overlook an assumption, or reject a valid step.

Line 5 of a proof that every function on a negation-closed domain is an even function plus an odd function asserts

$f(x)=f(-x)+(-f(x))$

The constant function $f\equiv 1$ turns it into $1=0$. The theorem is true; this step is not. Check it yourself below.

One objection, inspected

A true theorem, a false step

When a critic challenges a step in a proof with a counterexample, two distinct questions arise: is the counterexample mathematically valid as formalized, and does that formal statement represent the claim in the source? Together, a formal counterexample checked by Lean and a justified answer to the second question form a proof of error. The three tabs show the idea on the paper's own examples.

      Check the step

      This refutes the assertion at line 5; it says nothing against the theorem. The theorem itself holds: $f(x)=\tfrac{f(x)+f(-x)}{2}+\tfrac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2}$, and exchanging $x$ and $-x$ shows that the two summands are even and odd. A proof of error establishes that the named assertion fails under its stated assumptions; it does not establish that a proposed correction works.

      Exact saved witness and record facts

      A proposed witness can exhibit the claimed failure while violating one of the source's assumptions. In a Shellsort argument, the source assumes that a list's first entry is smaller than its last ($x_0<x_n$). Under that assumption it claims that if the first gap-$m$ insertion swaps the first entry with the entry $m$ positions later, that exchanged-in entry remains first in the final $m$-sorted list. The first witness, $(3,4,2,5,1)$ with $m=2$, has a proof checked by Lean, yet $3<1$ is false: it violates the endpoint assumption. The replacement $(3,5,2,6,1,7,4)$ satisfies $3<4$, refutes the same step, and also has a Lean certificate. BugBank keeps both versions and the reason for the replacement.

      Step through the gap-2 insertion pass on either input: entries two positions apart are compared and exchanged when out of order; the exchange that writes position 0 is the one the source's claim is about. The replacement theorem proves the endpoint inequality, the exchange hypothesis and the failed conclusion together (Appendix C.3 of the paper).

      A printed algorithm for the integer square root $\lfloor\sqrt{x}\rfloor$ starts with answer $0$ at $0$; given answer $b$ at $x-1$, it returns $b$ if $x\le(b+1)^2$ and $b+1$ otherwise. At $x=1$ the weak guard keeps the answer $0$, so the algorithm returns $0$ instead of $1$. Asking why turns one failure into the exact failure set: for every positive input the printed algorithm returns $\lfloor\sqrt{x-1}\rfloor$, lagging the true value by one input, and it is wrong exactly at the positive perfect squares. Replacing $\le$ by $<$, the correction the source proposed, removes the delay.       What a dossier contains, and what it establishes

      Each objection dossier preserves four groups of evidence for inspection. Select a group for its definition; the table beneath follows the active tab and shows which of the three conditions of a proof of error hold for that example.

      A formal certificate proves that the encoded assumptions hold and the encoded conclusion fails at the witness. Two witness-local implications connect it to the source: the encoded assumptions must imply the source's assumptions at the witness, and the source's conclusion at the witness must imply the encoded conclusion. A positive review of source correspondence is an interpretive judgment, separate from Lean's kernel check, and is not itself a formal proof.

      How a dossier is built

      Six stages, four possible search outcomes

      Given a problem statement and a numbered proof, Co-PEER constructs an objection dossier in six stages. Prompts, search bounds and procedures are given in the paper's appendix on procedures and executable checks.

      1. Selecting a candidate assertion

        Two calls to a fixed language model each select an assertion that may admit a short counterexample, quoting the source verbatim with its assumptions and conclusion; a deterministic validator checks the quotation and format, and a second model reviews each candidate.

      2. Searching for a counterexample

        A search agent writes and runs exact-arithmetic programs and records one of four outcomes: a concrete witness, no witness found after exhaustive search of a specified finite set, an inconclusive result, or a claim outside the permitted search setting.

      3. Checking the witness independently

        A separately written checker program reconstructs the condition from the quoted claim and tests the witness independently of the search script.

      4. Formalizing the certificate in Lean

        Human contributors and coding agents construct the formal certificate; Lean checks the proof, and an axiom audit records the assumptions and dependencies used.

      5. Reviewing the mathematics and source correspondence

        Three fresh model reviews: one searches for a counterexample from the claim alone, one tests the recorded witness with its own program, one compares the Lean certificate with the quoted claim and witness; a further model triages flagged records (keep, repair, exclude, or refer to human review).

      6. Recording the dossier

        The workflow packages the stage inputs, outputs, certificates, and any recorded review decisions into a persistent objection dossier.

      Search outcomes across 604 executable tasks

      A failure to find a counterexample does not establish a claim: an exhaustive search verifies it only over the finite domain checked.

      Human review

      Reviewers answered three questions for each dossier they examined: does the dossier read the source claim correctly (Q1); is the counterexample valid under the source's assumptions (Q2); does the Lean statement correspond to the claim and witness (Q3)? The core contains the dossiers with recorded all-YES answers and current version continuity. In the final stratified audit of 100 dossiers drawn from the 284 retained ones, with earlier verdicts masked, 91 were approved, a weighted 91.0% (95% interval 81.0% to 96.5%); the nine nonapprovals are excluded from the core. The rounds have different populations and purposes, and counts are reviews of the listed artifacts, not unique dossiers.

      Evaluating critics

      Can a model point to the line that is wrong?

      In certified-claim localization a critic receives a problem statement and a numbered proof text that is known to contain an error, without the counterexample or the certificate, and returns up to three ranked line numbers with a brief explanation. A reference line is one of the input's accepted error lines, backed by a core dossier. Hit@1 counts texts whose first choice is a reference line; Hit@3 counts a reference line among the first three choices. Responses that break the format, budget or execution rules count as misses.

      The strongest evaluated critic, Gemini 3.1 Pro, selects a reference line as its first choice in 73.0% of the 100 primary texts, against 18.0% for the best of four simple text rules; two configurations reach 85.0% within three choices. This is reference-line selection on texts known to contain an error. It is not general proof-verification accuracy and not a false-accusation rate, and a separate review of saved responses shows that some responses that select a reference line do not supply a valid objection while some that miss it do. Human approval covers all core dossiers; the exact current line labels were reviewed separately on smaller samples.

      From a failure to the mathematics

      Asking why a counterexample works

      A checked counterexample does more than stop a broken proof. Asking why the witness works can identify missing hypotheses, guide a repair, and lead to theorems over an entire family. The initial graph and integer-colouring witnesses came from model runs asked to prove or refute the source statements without access to BugBank; with the help of AI tools, we developed the classifications, the optimality proof and the further certified families.

      Paper, code and citation

      Co-PEER: Auditing Mathematics with Proofs of Error

      Abstract

      An objection to a mathematical proof requires evidence of its own: a critic can misread a claim, overlook an assumption, or reject a valid step. Counterexamples provide checkable evidence by refuting a false assertion even inside a proof of a true theorem. We introduce Co-PEER, a workflow for constructing and checking proofs of error. Each objection dossier records the challenged source claim, its assumptions, and a concrete counterexample. It includes a Lean-checked proof that the encoded assumptions hold and the encoded conclusion fails, and a review of whether the formal statement represents the source claim. BugBank stores these dossiers and their revision histories. A human-approved core of 147 dossiers drawn from 137 distinct sources supports a benchmark of 100 proof texts known to contain an error. The strongest evaluated LLM critic selects a reference line (a line annotated as incorrect) as its first choice in 73.0% of texts, compared with 18.0% for the best of four simple text rules. We also test whether a checked counterexample helps models complete Lean repair tasks in which the correction to prove is given. Finally, with the help of AI tools, we develop selected counterexamples into explanations of entire families of failures. We characterize and count the frozen six-colorings of doubled odd cycles of length at least five. We prove that a two-threshold coloring of the integers is the unique optimum within its family and exceeds a conjectured bound. We also determine every input on which a printed integer square-root algorithm fails and prove the correction proposed in the source.

      Language models contributed to source triage, counterexample search, executable checkers, Lean development, semantic reviews, qualitative coding, experimental grading, mathematical follow-ups, and manuscript revision; the paper's AI-use statement records their contributions and the human responsibilities.

This result is relevant to the study of the relational complexity of structures defined by forbidden embeddings.

35 | \section{Introduction}
36 | 	A countable structure relational structure $M$ for the relational language $L=\{R_i:i\in I\}$ is \emph{(ultra)homogeneous} if any isomorphism between finite substructures extends to an automorphism of $M$. Countable structures homogeneous in a finite relational language $L$ are $\omega$-categorical and eliminate quantifiers in the language $L$, so the orbit of a tuple $\bar a$ is determined by the atomic diagram of $\bar a$. Fra\"iss\'e's Theorem states that homogeneous structures are in correspondence with amalgamation classes of finite structures.
37 | 
38 | 	Homogeneous graphs are well known: in the finite case, they are (up to complementation) unions of cliques, complete bipartite graphs $K_{n,n}$, the pentagon, and the line graph of $K_{3,3}$ (Gardiner \cite{gardiner1976homogeneous}); the infinite homogeneous graphs are (again, up to complementation) unions of isomorphic cliques, the universal homogeneous $K_n$-free graphs, and the Rado graph (Lachlan-Woodrow \cite{lachlan1980countable}). In a couple of papers, Covington presented a criterion that implies homogenizability in a finite relational language, the main hypothesis of which is the local failure of amalgamation for the class of finite structures, \ie having only finitely many minimal amalgamation problems without a solution. The converse of Covington's Theorem is false: for example, the class of finite bipartite graphs does not have local failure of amalgamation, but becomes an amalgamation class when the language is extended to include an equivalence relation that is interpreted as a bipartition of the underlying graph.
39 | 
40 | 	Homogeneity (or even homogenizability) is not preserved under interpretation. For example, it is easy to prove that the action of the automorphism group of the Rado graph on the set of edges is not homogenizable in any finite relational language (equivalently: the line graph of the Rado graph is not homogenizable).
41 | 
42 | 	The line graph $\gts$ of the universal homogeneous triangle-free graph $\gt$ seems to be placed ideally as a case study for homogenizable structures defined by forbidden embeddings: it is not a homogeneous graph (Lachlan-Woodrow); it is also not easy to homogenize, in the sense that we cannot apply Covington's Theorem (see \cite{covington1989universal} and \cite{covington1990homogenizable}, more on this in Section \ref{SectEnto}); and moreover, the Hartman-Hubi\v cka-Ne\v set\v ril theory developed in \cite{hartman2015complexities} to estimate the relational complexity of structures defined by forbidden homomorphisms cannot be applied here. For these reasons, a study of line graphs from the perspective of relational complexity or homogenizability seems useful.

84 | \begin{proposition}\label{PropEquiv}
85 | Let $G=(V(G),E(G))$ be a $\kk$-free graph. Then the relation $\sim$ on $E(G)$ given by $e\sim e'$ if the endpoints of $e,e'$ span a clique in $G$ is an equivalence relation. The $\sim$ classes are cliques, and two distinct classes are either disjoint or have exactly one vertex in common.
86 | \end{proposition}
87 | \begin{proof}
88 | Reflexivity and symmetry are clear. To prove transitivity, suppose that $e_1\sim e_2\sim e_3$. If any two of them are equal, transitivity follows trivially, so assume the edges are distinct. If $e_1\cap e_2\neq\varnothing$ and $e_2\cap e_3\neq\varnothing$, then $|e_1\cup e_2\cup e_3|=4$ and there are at least five edges in total, so there must be six by $\kk$-freeness. Suppose then that $e_1\cap e_2=\varnothing$; since the edges are distinct, $e_3$ has an endpoint $u$ not in $e_2$. The endpoints of $e_2$ together with $u$ and any endpoint of $e_1$ must form a $K_4$, since the conditions $e_1\sim e_2\sim e_3$ imply at least five edges on those four vertices. It is clear from the definition of $\sim$ that for any $e$, $\cup e/\sim$ is a clique in $G$.
89 | 
90 | Let $C, C'$ be two $\sim$ classes. Suppose $\{u,v\}\subset C\cap C'$. If $C=C'=\{u,v\}$, then we are done, so suppose $C$ contains a vertex $w\notin\{u,v\}$. Then $w\in C'$, since $w$ forms a triangle with $u,v\in C'$, and similarly any vertex in $C'$ is also in $C$, so the classes are equal. It follows that $|C\cap C'|\leq1$.
91 | \end{proof}

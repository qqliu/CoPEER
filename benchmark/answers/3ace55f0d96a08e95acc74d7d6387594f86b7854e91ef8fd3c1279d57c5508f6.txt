The claim is: F_{m+n} = F_m F_{n+1} + F_{m-1} F_n, for m>=1, n>=0, with F_0=0, F_1=1. The combinatorial proof: number of ways to climb m+n-1 stairs with steps 1 or 2 is F_{m+n}. Now, they split the ways based on the first time the climber reaches exactly m-1 stairs or exactly m-2 stairs and then takes a step of two. But is this a valid partition? Let's try to see if there is a way to climb m+n-1 stairs that does not fit either category, or fits both. The statement: "we can either climb the stairs and at some point having climbed exactly m-1 stairs, or we can climb the stairs and at some point having climbed exactly m-2 stairs, after which we take a step of two stairs". This suggests that in any valid climbing, either you never take a step of two stairs after reaching m-2, or you do. But the phrasing is ambiguous. Let's attempt to construct a counterexample to the splitting. Take m=2, n=2: we already did, works. m=3, n=3: m+n-1=5 stairs. Ways F_6=8.

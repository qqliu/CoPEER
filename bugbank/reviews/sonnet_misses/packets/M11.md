      0094 | \section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
      0095 | Spanning tree $T$ of a simple connected graph $G$ is a connected subgraph of $G$ having all vertices and exactly $n-1$ edges of $G$. There can be many spanning trees of a connected graph. In his 1847 seminal paper \cite{kirchhoff1847}, Gustav Robert Kirchhoff showed how it is possible to count the number of spanning trees in a connected graph from its \hyperref[sec:adjm_incm]{incidence matrix}. Since the incidence matrix is linked to the \hyperref[sec:laplacian]{combinatorial Laplacian matrix} of the graph, the number of spanning trees in of the graph is linked to the eigenvalues of the combinatorial Laplacian matrix, that is, to the roots of the \hyperref[sec:char_poly]{characteristic polynomial} of the combinatorial Laplacian matrix. This is an elegant result which combines concepts from spectral graph theory (eigenvalues of graph matrices), linear algebra (determinants and matrices), graph theory (spanning trees), and combinatorics (counting). The proof touches only simple concepts and formulas from linear algebra which a $12^{th}$ grader should understand. Moreover, the graph theory concepts used by the proof are taught in first few lectures in graph theory. Therefore the elegance of the theorem (and its proof) lies in connecting so many unrelated dots and providing a deep result.

      0293 | The sign of the cofactor $C_{ii}$ for any diagonal element $m_{ii}$ will be $(-1)^{i+i} = (-1)^{2i} = +1$. Therefore, we have $C_{ii} = [M]_{i,i}$.
      0294 | \end{remark}

      0296 | \subsection{Laplace Expansion}\label{sec:laplace}
      0297 | 
      0298 | Let us recall the Leibnitz formula from Equation~\eqref{eq:leibnitz} for determinant of the matrix $M$:
      0299 | \begin{eqnarray*}
      0300 | det\left(M\right)
      0301 | &=& \sum_{\sigma \in S_n}{ sgn(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n}{m_{i\sigma(i)}} }\,.
      0302 | 
      0303 | \end{eqnarray*}
      0304 | 
      0305 | Each product term includes $n$ elements from $M$, each from a different row and different column. Let us consider the set of product terms $P=\{p\}$ that contain $m_{ij}$ as a factor. Let $\sigma$ be a permutation of the numbers $1,2,\cdots,n$ associated with $p$. Then,
      0306 | 
      0307 | \begin{eqnarray}
      0308 | p
      0309 | &=& sgn(\sigma)m_{1\sigma(1)} m_{2\sigma(2)}\cdots m_{i\sigma(i)}\cdots m_{n\sigma(n)}\nonumber \\
      0310 | &=& sgn(\sigma)m_{1\sigma(1)} m_{2\sigma(2)}\cdots \underbrace{m_{ij}}_{\sigma(i)=j}\cdots m_{n\sigma(n)}\nonumber \\
      0311 | &=& (sgn(\sigma)m_{ij}) m_{1\sigma(1)}m_{2\sigma(2)}\cdots m_{(i-1)\sigma(i-1)}m_{(i+1)\sigma(i+1)} \cdots m_{n\sigma(n)} \nonumber \\
      0312 | &=& (sgn(\sigma)m_{ij}) \prod_{\substack{1\leq k \leq n\\k \neq i}}{m_{k\sigma(k)}}\,.
      0313 | \label{eq:laplace_exp_ij}
      0314 | \end{eqnarray}
      0315 | 
      0316 | Let $\pi$ be a permutation on the base sequence
      0317 | \begin{eqnarray}
      0318 | \pi_b = 1,2,\cdots,i-1,i+1,\cdots,n
      0319 | \label{eq:}
      0320 | \end{eqnarray}.
      0321 | 
      0322 | That is, $\pi$ is a permutation of the numbers $\{k:1\leq k \leq n, k \neq i\}$ and the base permutation is $\pi_b$. $\pi$ is related to $\sigma$ as follows:
      0323 | \begin{eqnarray}
      0324 | \pi(k) &=& \left\{\begin{array}{l}
      0325 | 	\sigma(k), \mbox{ if } k < i \\
      0326 | 	\sigma(k+1), \mbox{ if } i \leq k \leq n-1
      0327 | \end{array}\right.\,.
      0328 | \label{eq:laplace_exp_pi}
      0329 | \end{eqnarray}
      0330 | 
      0331 | Therefore there is a one-to-one correspondence between $\sigma$ and $\pi$ given $i$. Figure~\ref{fig:sigma_pi_mij} depicts this correspondence.
      0332 | 
      0333 | \begin{figure}
      0334 | \includegraphics[width=0.5\columnwidth]{sigma_pi_mij}
      0335 | \caption{One-to-one mapping for $\sigma$ to $\pi$, given $i$.}
      0336 | \label{fig:sigma_pi_mij}
      0337 | \end{figure}
      0338 | 
      0339 | Now if we want to bring the element $j$ in $\sigma$ from its current position $i$ to the new position $j$, we will have to make $|j-i|$ position interchanges on $\sigma$. Let $\rho$ be the resultant permutation with the following properties:
      0340 | 
      0341 | \begin{eqnarray}
      0342 | dist(\rho) &=& dist(\sigma) + |j-i|
      0343 | \label{eq:laplace_exp_dist_rho}
      0344 | \end{eqnarray}
      0345 | 
      0346 | and
      0347 | 
      0348 | \begin{eqnarray}
      0349 | \rho(j) &=& j \\
      0350 | \pi(k) &=& \left\{\begin{array}{l}
      0351 | 	\rho(k), k < j \\
      0352 | 	\rho(k+1), j \leq k \leq n-1
      0353 | \end{array}\right. \\
      0354 | \label{eq:laplace_exp_rho}
      0355 | \end{eqnarray}
      0356 | Here, $dist()$ is the distance function (see Definition~\ref{def:perm_dist}).
      0357 | 
      0358 | Since $\rho(j)=j$, the element $j$ is already in its position as in the base permutation. Additionally, if we delete the position $j$ from $\rho$ we get $\pi$. Thus it follows that
      0359 | \begin{eqnarray}
      0360 | dist(\pi) &=& dist(\rho)
      0361 | \label{eq:laplace_exp_dist_rho_pi}
      0362 | \end{eqnarray}
      0363 | 
      0364 | By combining Equation~\ref{eq:laplace_exp_dist_rho} and Equation~\ref{eq:laplace_exp_dist_rho_pi}, we get
      0365 | \begin{eqnarray}
      0366 | 		dist(\pi) &=& dist(\sigma) + |j-i|\,,
      0367 | 		\end{eqnarray}
      0368 | 
      0369 | which implies
      0370 | 
      0371 | 	\begin{eqnarray}
      0372 | 		sgn(\sigma) &=& sgn(\pi) (-1)^{|j-i|}\,.
      0373 | 		\label{eq:laplace_exp_dist_sigma}
      0374 | 		\end{eqnarray}
      0375 | 
      0376 | We note that \begin{eqnarray}
      0377 | (-1)^{|j-i|}
      0378 | &=& (-1)^{j-i} \nonumber \\
      0379 | &=& (-1)^{i+j- 2i} \nonumber \\
      0380 | &=& (-1)^{i+j}\,.
      0381 | \label{eq:laplace_exp_minus_j_i}
      0382 | \end{eqnarray}
      0383 | 
      0384 | Then, Equation~\eqref{eq:laplace_exp_dist_sigma} becomes
      0385 | \begin{eqnarray}
      0386 | sgn(\sigma) &=& sgn(\pi) (-1)^{i+j}\,.
      0387 | \label{eq:laplace_exp_sgn_sigma_final}
      0388 | \end{eqnarray}
      0389 | 
      0390 | Now consider Equation~\eqref{eq:laplace_exp_ij}. By substituting $\sigma(k)$ with $\pi(k)$ as defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:laplace_exp_pi}, and by substituting $sgn(\sigma)$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:laplace_exp_sgn_sigma_final}, we get
      0391 | \begin{eqnarray}
      0392 | p &=& (-1)^{i+j}m_{ij} \left(sgn(\pi) \prod_{\substack{1\leq k \leq n\\k \neq i}}{m_{k \pi(k)}}\right)
      0393 | \label{eq:laplace_exp_p_pi}
      0394 | \end{eqnarray}
      0395 | 
      0396 | Therefore, the sum of all product terms $p$ that involves $m_{ij}$ as factor is
      0397 | \begin{eqnarray}
      0398 | \sum{p}
      0399 | &=&
      0400 | \sum{(-1)^{i+j}m_{ij} \left(sgn(\pi) \prod_{\substack{1\leq k \leq n\\k \neq i}}{m_{k \pi(k)}}\right)} \nonumber \\
      0401 | &=& (-1)^{i+j}m_{ij}\sum{sgn(\pi) \prod_{\substack{1\leq k \leq n\\k \neq i}}{m_{k \pi(k)}}} \nonumber \\
      0402 | &=& (-1)^{i+j}m_{ij}[M]_{i,j}\nonumber \\
      0403 | &=& m_{ij}C_{ij}\,,
      0404 | \label{eq:laplace_exp_final_mij}
      0405 | \end{eqnarray}
      0406 | 
      0407 | where $[M]_{i,j}$ is the minor of the element $m_{ij}$, and $C_{ij}$ is the $(i,j)$th cofactor of $M$. Figure~\ref{fig:minor_11} depicts the product terms that contain $m_{11}$. $m_{11}$ is colored green. The Row and the column of $m_{11}$ is colored gray since these factors do not appear in $p \in P$. The white area corresponds to the principal minor of $m_{11}$.
      0408 | 
      0409 | \begin{figure}
      0410 | \includegraphics[width=0.3\columnwidth]{minor_11}
      0411 | \caption{The minor of $m_{11}$ shown in white. Gray cells denote the row and column corresponding to $m_{11}$ that do not contribute to the minor of $m_{11}$.}
      0412 | \label{fig:minor_11}
      0413 | \end{figure}
      0414 | 
      0415 | 
      0416 | The sum in Equation~\eqref{eq:laplace_exp_final_mij} is the sum of product terms that contain $m_{ij}$ as factor. The product terms in the expression of $det(M)$ can be partitioned into $n$ groups corresponding to the factors $\{m_{ik}, 1 \leq k \leq n\}$ (elements from the same row). These $n$ factors will create $n$ partitions because no two factors can be present in the same product (since they come from the same row). Similar partitioning can be done using elmements from the same column ($\{m_{kj}, 1 \leq k \leq n\}$). Therefore, if we add up the sums corresponding to each partition, we will get $det(M)$. Hence we have the following theorem:
      0417 | 
      0418 | \begin{theorem}\label{theo:laplace_exp}
      0419 | (Laplace expansion of determinant)
      0420 | If $M$ is an $n\times n$ matrix,
      0421 | \begin{eqnarray*}
      0422 | det(M)
      0423 | &=& \sum_{k=1}^n{m_{ik} C_{ik}} \mbox {  (along row } i \mbox{)} \nonumber \\
      0424 | &=& \sum_{k=1}^n{m_{kj} C_{kj}} \mbox {  (along column } j \mbox{)} \nonumber \\
      0425 | \mbox{)}\nonumber
      0426 | \label{eq:laplace_expansion}
      0427 | \end{eqnarray*}
      0428 | \end{theorem}
      0429 | 
      0430 | \begin{proof}
      0431 | Presented in the preceding arguments.
      0432 | \end{proof}
      0433 | 
      0434 | Thus the Laplace expansion gives a recursive formula for determinant, since each cofactors is the determinant of an $(n-1)\times (n-1)$ submatrix of $M$ (with appropriate $\pm$ sign).
      0435 | 
      0436 | 
      0437 | For example, given the matrix
      0438 | \[M=\left(
      0439 | 	\begin{array}{ccc}
      0440 | 		1 & 2 & 3 \\
      0441 | 		6 & 5 & 4 \\
      0442 | 		9 & 7 & 8
      0443 | 	\end{array}
      0444 | \right)\,,\]
      0445 | the determinant is
      0446 | 
      0447 | \begin{eqnarray*}
      0448 | 	det(M) &=& 	m_{11} \times C_{11} + m_{12} \times C_{12} + m_{13} \times C_{13} \\
      0449 | 					&=&	1 \times C_{11} + 2 \times C_{12} + 3 \times C_{13} \\
      0450 | 					&=&	1 \times (-1)^{1+1}(40-28) + 2 \times (-1)^{1+2}(48-36) + 3 \times (-1)^{1+3}(42-45) \\
      0451 | 					&=& 1 \times 12 + 2 \times (-12) + 3 \times (-3) \\
      0452 | 					&=& 12 - 24 -9\\
      0453 | 					&=& -21
      0454 | \end{eqnarray*}
      0455 | 
      0456 | \begin{remark}\label{rem:det_a_transpose}
      0457 | The determinant can be expanded along any row or column. Expanding $det(M)$ along a row of $M$ is the same as expanding $det(M^T)$ along the corresponding column. Therefore, $det(M)=det(M^T)$.
      0458 | \end{remark}

      0566 | \subsubsection{Duplicate Rows or Columns}
      0567 | 
      0568 | \begin{proposition}\label{prop:det_dup_cols}
      0569 | (Determinant with Duplicate Rows/Columns)
      0570 | Suppose we replace the column $C_i$ (or row $R_i$) of the matrix $M$ with another column $C_j$ (or row $R_j$) such that the derived matrix $\widehat{M}$ has two duplicate columns (or rows). Then, $det(\widehat{M})=0$.
      0571 | \end{proposition}
      0572 | 
      0573 | \begin{proof}
      0574 | Let $\widehat{M}^\prime$ be the matrix obtained from $\widehat{M}$ by exchanging the duplicate columns (of rows) of $\widehat{M}$. By Proposition~\autoref{prop:det_switch_col}, we have
      0575 | \[
      0576 | det(\widehat{M}^\prime) = - det(\widehat{M})\,.
      0577 | \]
      0578 | However, the matrices $\widehat{M}^\prime$ and $\widehat{M}$ are the same because we interchanged two identical columns (or rows). Therefore,
      0579 | \[
      0580 | det(\widehat{M}^\prime) = det(\widehat{M})\,.
      0581 | \]
      0582 | The only way the above two equalities hold true is when
      0583 | \[
      0584 | det(\widehat{M}) = 0\,.
      0585 | \]
      0586 | \end{proof}

      0589 | \subsubsection{Interchange\slash Addition\slash Multiplication of Rows or Columns}
      0590 | 
      0591 | Below we will define \textit{elementary operations} on a matrix $M$, then we ask the following question: Let $\widehat{M}$ be the matrix obtained by applied a sequence of elementary row/column operations on $M$. What is the relationship between $det(\widehat{M})$ and $det(M)$?
      0592 | 
      0593 | \begin{definition}
      0594 | Let $R_i$ and $C_i$ be the $i$th row and $i$th column of the matrix $M$, respectively. An elementary row/column operation on $M$ can be one of the following three operations on its rows/columns.
      0595 | \begin{enumerate}
      0596 | 	\item \textbf{$O_1$: Row (Column) swithcing} Interhanging two rows (columns) within the matrix $M$.
      0597 | 	\[
      0598 | 		R_i \leftrightarrow R_j\,.
      0599 | 		C_i \leftrightarrow C_j\,.
      0600 | 	\]
      0601 | 	\item \textbf{$O_2$: Row (Column) multiplication} Each element of a row (column) is multiplied by a scalar $k$.
      0602 | 	\[
      0603 | 		kR_i \rightarrow R_i, k\neq 0\,.
      0604 | 		kC_i \rightarrow C_i, k\neq 0\,.
      0605 | 	\]
      0606 | 	\item \textbf{$O_3$: Row (Column) addition} Each row (column) can be replaced with the sum of that row (column) and a scalar multiple of another row (column).
      0607 | 	\[
      0608 | 		R_i + kR_j \rightarrow R_i, k\neq 0\,.
      0609 | 		C_i + kC_j \rightarrow C_i, k\neq 0\,.
      0610 | 	\]
      0611 | \end{enumerate}
      0612 | \end{definition}
      0613 | 
      0614 | \begin{proposition}\label{prop:det_switch_col}
      0615 | (Determinant under Row/Column exchange)
      0616 | Suppose we exchange the columns $C_i$ and $C_j$ (or rows $R_i$ and $R_j$) of the matrix $M$. Let $\widehat{M}$ be the resultant matrix. Then,
      0617 | \[
      0618 | det(\widehat{M}) = -det(M)\,.
      0619 | \]
      0620 | \end{proposition}
      0621 | \begin{proof}
      0622 | The columns (or rows) of $M$ are in their base permutation (that is, in their original position). Let $\pi$ be the column (or row) permutation of $\widehat{M}$ after making one column (or row) interchange. Thus $dist(\pi) = 1$. Thus permutation of each product term in $det(\widehat{M})$ will be $dist(\pi)$ away from the permutation of the same product term in $det(M)$. However, $dist(\pi) = 1$ means $sgn(\pi)=-1$ (by Definition~\ref{def:perm_sign}). Therefore, by definition,
      0623 | \begin{eqnarray}
      0624 | det(\widehat{M})
      0625 | &=& \sum_{\sigma}{sgn(\sigma) \prod_{i}{\widehat{m}_{i\sigma(i)}}} \nonumber \\
      0626 | &=& \sum_{\sigma}{sgn(\sigma)sgn(\pi) \prod_{i}{m_{i\sigma(i)}}} \nonumber \\
      0627 | &=& sgn(\pi) \sum_{\sigma}{sgn(\sigma)\prod_{i}{m_{i\sigma(i)}}} \nonumber \\
      0628 | &=& sgn(\pi) det(M) \nonumber \\
      0629 | &=& -det(M)
      0630 | \label{eq:det_elem_op_row_exchange}
      0631 | \end{eqnarray}
      0632 | \end{proof}
      0633 |

      0750 | \begin{definition}\label{def:linear_dep}(Linear Dependence)
      0751 | Let $R=\{(R)_1,(R)_2,\cdots,(R)_n\}$ be a set of vectors. Then, the vectors  $\in R$ are linearly dependent if there exists a linear combination of the vectors $(R)_i,1\leq i\leq n$ which equals to zero. That is, if
      0752 | \begin{eqnarray}
      0753 | \sum_{\substack{j\\i \neq j}}{a_j(R)_j} &=& 0
      0754 | \end{eqnarray}
      0755 | for some scalars $a_1,a_2,\cdots,a_n$.
      0756 | \end{definition}

      0759 | \begin{eqnarray}

      0761 | 1 & 2 & 4 \\
      0762 | 3 & 2 & 8 \\
      0763 | 5 & 1 & 11 \\
      0764 | \end{bmatrix}\,.
      0765 | \label{eq:mat_lin_dep}

      1032 | \subsection{Singular Matrix}
      1033 | If the determinant of a matrix is zero, the matrix is called \textit{singular} and by definition, it cannot not have an inverse (hence it is not \textit{invertible}).
      1034 |

      1041 | Presented in the preceding arguments.

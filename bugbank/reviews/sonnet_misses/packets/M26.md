      0277 | 		\left| \langle a_1^{m_1}, a_2^{m_2}, \dots, a_i^{m_i} \rangle \right| = m.
      0278 | 		\]
      0279 | 	\end{proof}

      0281 | 	\begin{theorem}\label{corthm}
      0282 | 		The algorithm \textsc{FindSubgroup} terminates and returns a subgroup $H$ of order $m$.
      0283 | 	\end{theorem}
      0284 | 
      0285 | 	\begin{proof}
      0286 | 		The algorithm ensures that in each iteration, the number of remaining elements in $G'$ strictly decreases, guaranteeing termination. By Proposition \ref{ordthm} and Lemma \ref{corlem}, the final subgroup $H$ always has order $m$.
      0287 | 	\end{proof}

      0289 | 	\subsection{Complexity Analysis of the Algorithm}
      0290 | 
      0291 | 	To determine the complexity, we analyze the number of iterations and the time required to compute the values $m_j$ in lines 14 and 20 of the algorithm. Note that $m_j$ in line 14 can be set to $k_j$, specifically as
      0292 | 	\[
      0293 | 	m_j = \frac{k_j}{\gcd(k_j, k/m)}.
      0294 | 	\]
      0295 | 	From this, we observe that
      0296 | 	\[
      0297 | 	\left[ \gcd(k_1, k/m), \gcd(k_2, k/m), \dots, \gcd(k_i, k/m) \right] = k/m.
      0298 | 	\]
      0299 | 	Since the number of distinct values $m_j$ cannot exceed $m$, their computation requires $\tilde{O}(m)$ time, where $\tilde{O}(m)$ accounts for the logarithmic time taken in the associated gcd computations.
      0300 | 
      0301 | 	The total time required to compute $\langle a \rangle$ in line 4 across all iterations is $O(k)$, since each cyclic subgroup $\langle a \rangle$ is computed at most once and subsequently removed from $G'$.
      0302 | 
      0303 | 	Next, the total time for subgroup computations in line 10 across all iterations is given by
      0304 | 	\[
      0305 | 	k_1 + k_1 k_2 + \dots + k,
      0306 | 	\]
      0307 | 	where $k_i$ are (not necessarily distinct) prime factors of $k$. This sum is also bounded by $O(k)$.
      0308 | 
      0309 | 	Furthermore, the total number of divisors of $k$, denoted by $d(k)$, satisfies the bound \cite{div}
      0310 | 	\[
      0311 | 	d(k) \leq k^{\left( \frac{1.06601...}{\log\log k} \right)}.
      0312 | 	\]
      0313 | 	Thus, the overall runtime of the algorithm is $O(k)$. Additionally, the time required to compute the orders of all elements in the group can also be bounded by $O(k)$, as noted in \cite{kavitha}.
      0314 | 
      0315 | 	\begin{remark}
      0316 | 		The complexity of the subgroup-finding problem exhibits a stark contrast between abelian and non-abelian groups. Our NP-completeness reduction relies on the structure of the symmetric group $S_n$, where elements do not necessarily commute, allowing for the encoding of the Hamiltonian Cycle problem. In contrast, for abelian groups, where every element commutes, we present a linear-time algorithm. This contrast highlights how structural properties of groups dramatically impact computational complexity: while the problem remains intractable in the general (non-abelian) case, it becomes efficiently solvable in the abelian setting.
      0317 | 	\end{remark}

      0319 | 	\subsection{Complexity Contrast: Abelian vs. Non-Abelian Groups}
      0320 | 	Our results demonstrate a striking complexity difference in the problem of finding a subgroup of a specified order: the problem is NP-complete in the general case but admits a linear-time algorithm when restricted to abelian groups. This sharp contrast is not unique to our setting and appears in various computational problems in group theory.
      0321 | 	\begin{itemize}
      0322 | 		\item Group Isomorphism: Isomorphism testing for abelian groups can be done in polynomial time, whereas for non-abelian groups, the best-known algorithms are significantly slower and the problem remains a major open question.
      0323 | 		\item Word Problem: In abelian groups, the word problem is solvable in polynomial time using linear algebra techniques. In contrast, for general non-abelian groups, it can be undecidable.
      0324 | 		\item Conjugacy Problem: Trivial in abelian groups but can be computationally hard in non-abelian settings.
      0325 | 		\item Finding Normal Subgroups: Immediate in abelian groups, where every subgroup is normal, but potentially hard in non-abelian groups.
      0326 | 		\item Commutator Problems: Trivial in abelian groups (since all elements commute) but non-trivial and computationally challenging in general.
      0327 | 	\end{itemize}
      0328 | 
      0329 | 	These examples suggest that the presence of commutativity significantly simplifies computational problems, whereas the added structure in non-abelian groups often leads to increased complexity. Given our results, it would be interesting to explore whether similar complexity gaps exist for other group-theoretic problems under different representations.

      0331 | 	\section{Conclusion}
      0332 | 	In this paper, we studied the problem of determining whether a finite group contains a subgroup of a given order. We established that this problem is NP-complete in the general case by providing a reduction from the Hamiltonian cycle problem. Additionally, we presented a linear-time algorithm for abelian groups when given their Cayley table representation. This highlights a significant complexity gap between abelian and non-abelian cases, reinforcing the role of group structure in computational tractability.
      0333 | 
      0334 | 	Our results contribute to the broader understanding of computational group theory, particularly in the context of different group representations. An interesting direction for future work is to explore the parameterized complexity of the problem for various structural restrictions, such as nilpotent or solvable groups. Additionally, understanding the complexity of related problems, such as finding cyclic subgroups of a given order, remains an open avenue for further research.

65 | \begin{abstract}
66 | 
67 |     We study high dimensional expansion beyond simplicial complexes (posets) and focus on $q$-complexes which are complexes whose basic building blocks are linear spaces. We show that the complete $q$-complex (consists of all subspaces of a given linear space) may have non-trivial homology groups and therefore some techniques for simplicial complexes fail. We develop new techniques to work bypass this. In particular:
68 | 
69 |     \begin{enumerate}
70 |         \item We describe a new construction of cones and use it to determine when the homology of the complete $q$-complex is trivial. We use this construction to prove the ''projective support dimension conjecture'' conjectured by Mnukhin and Siemons \cite{MNUKHIN}.
71 | 
72 |         \item We define topological high dimensional expansion for posets, and show that the complete $q$-complex has linear (in the number of lines) coboundary expansion.
73 | 
74 |         \item We define the $q$-Linial-Meshulam model of random $q$ complexes and prove a sharp threshold for the connectivity of random $q$-complexes.
75 |     \end{enumerate}
76 | \end{abstract}

79 | \section{Introduction}
80 | 
81 | In recent years, a theory of ''high dimensional expanders'' has emerged, and been successfully applied in mathematics and computer science to resolve longstanding open problems (\cite{LTCPaper}, \cite{LogConcave}). Another thread of research is using the geometry of vector spaces to obtain better construction in TCS, most notably, in the seminal works  \cite{UGC3}, \cite{UGC2} a weaker variant of the unique games conjecture was reduced to an expansion problem on the Grassmann graph which was later solved in \cite{UGC1}. The Grassmann graph is a graph whose vertices are the $\ell$ dimensional linear spaces of some $\mathbb{F}_q^n$ and two subspaces $U, W$ are connected by an edge if $dim(U \cap W)=\ell-1$.
82 | 
83 | In this work we initiate the study of coboundary high-dimensional expansion on structures beyond simplicial complexes (in contrast to spectral expansion which was studied in \cite{KTTensors}), and mainly specialize in understanding $q$-complexes (see \Cref{sec : setting} for definition) which is a collection of linear subspaces of some space $V$ that are closed under inclusion.
84 | This gives rise to a boundary map, for which the boundary of a subspace $U$ is the formal sum of all the subspaces of codimension $1$ contained in $U$.

87 | \subsection{The setting}\label{sec : setting}
88 | 
89 | Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space with $dim(V)=n$, over a finite field $
90 | \mathbb{F}_q$. Denote by $Gr(V)$ the set of all subspaces of $V$ and by $Gr_k(V)$ the set of all $k$ dimensional subspaces of $V$. We also use the notation $Gr(n)$ to denote $Gr(\mathbb{F}_q^n)$. Similarly define $Gr_k(n)=Gr_k(\mathbb{F}_q^n)$.
91 | 
92 | We remind that $|Gr_k(n)|$ is the Gaussian binomial coefficient, denoted by ${n \choose k}_q$ and satisfies:
93 | \begin{align*}
94 |     |Gr_k(V)|={n \choose k}_q = \frac{(q^n-1) \ldots (q^n-q^{k-1})}{(q^k-1) \ldots (q^k-q^{k-1})}
95 | \end{align*}

98 | \begin{definition}
99 |     A $q$-complex $X$ is a collection of \emph{subspaces} of some vector space $V$ closed under inclusion. That is: $ U \in X \implies Gr(U) \subseteq X$.
100 | \end{definition}
101 | 
102 | This should be thought of as a $q$-analog of simplicial complexes where the ''$q=1$ limit'' is simplicial complexes. One defines analogously the set of $k$ faces, $X(k)$ to be the set of all $k$-spaces in $X$.
103 | 
104 | Fix a ring $R$. A formal sum $\Sigma_{U \in X(k)} \alpha_U U$ with $\alpha_U \in R$ is a $k$-chain of $X$ over $R$. The space of all $k$ chains is an $R$-module denoted by $C_k(X)$. One can define a boundary map $\partial_k : C_k(X) \rightarrow C_{k-1}(X)$ by extending linearly:
105 | 
106 | \[ \partial_k(U)= \Sigma_{W \subseteq_1 U} W  \]
107 | 
108 | Where $W \subseteq_1 U$ denotes that $W$ is a subspace of co-dimension $1$ in $U$.
109 | 
110 | This boundary map was previously considered in the paper \cite{MNUKHIN} by Mnukhin and Siemons, who studied the homology of the complete $q$-complex (collection of all subspaces of a given fixed space $V$).
111 | 
112 | The space of $k$ cochains is defined by $C^k(X) = Hom(C_k(X),R)$, and the a coboundary operator $d_k:C^k(X) \rightarrow C^{k+1}(X)$ is the adjoint operator of $\partial_{k+1}$, and satisfies:
113 | 
114 | \[ d_k(\alpha)(U)=\alpha(\partial_{k+1}(U))\]
115 | 
116 |  If the complex is finite one can abuse the notation and consider $d_k: C_k(X) \rightarrow C_{k+1}(X)$ by associating $U \leftrightarrow \delta_U$ where $\delta_U$ is the cochain that satisfies: $\delta_U(W)= \delta_{U=W}$. where the R.H.S is the Kronecker delta function. We also use $\mathbf{0}$ to denote the $0$-chain supported on the zero space with coefficient one, that is $\mathbf{0} = 1 \cdot Span\{0\}$
117 | 
118 | We say $t \in \mathbb{Z}$ is zero in the unital ring $R$ if $t$ maps to zero under the natural map $\mathbb{Z}\to R.$  When the ring $R$ is suitable the map $\partial$ is indeed a boundary map:
119 | \begin{claim}\label{claim: delta squared}
120 |     $\partial^2=0$ if and only if $ (q+1)r = 0$ for all $r\in R$.
121 | \end{claim}
122 | 
123 | \begin{proof}
124 |     Let $[\alpha]_W$ denote the $W$ coordinate with respect to the standard basis of $C_k(X)$ of $\alpha$. Then:
125 | 
126 |     \[[\partial^2(r \cdot U)]_W=\#\{W \subseteq_1 Y \subseteq_1 U\} \cdot r \]
127 | 
128 |     By the correspondence theorem, this set is in bijection with lines in the space $\faktor{U}{W} \cong \mathbb{F}_q^2$. As there are $q+1$ such lines:\[[\partial^2(r \cdot U)]_W = (q+1)r  \]
129 | \end{proof}

131 | \begin{remark}
132 |     We use the notation $\partial^{(R)}$ to denote the operators $\partial$ when the chains and cochains are over a ring $R$ which may not satisfy $\left( \partial^{(R)}\right)^2=0$.
133 | \end{remark}
134 | 
135 | We can define (co)boundaries and (co)cycle in an analogous way to the definition in the theory of simplicial complexes. Elements of $Z_k(X,R)= ker(\partial_k)$ are cycles, and elements of $Z^k(X,R)= ker(d_k)$ are cocycles. $B_k=Im(\partial_{k+1})$ is the set of $k$-boundaries  and $B^k=Im(d_{k-1})$ is the set of $k$-coboundries.

141 | \subsection{Homology of $C_\bullet(Gr(V))$}
142 | 
143 | In \Cref{sec : homological properties} we study the homological properties of the complete $q$-complex $C_\bullet(Gr(V))$ for some $V$. We give a different, elementary, proof to a result of \cite{MNUKHIN} about the homology of the complete $q$-complex:
144 | 
145 | \begin{theorem}\label{thm - homologies tot}
146 |     Let $V$ be an $n$ dimensional space over $\mathbb{F}_q$ then:
147 |     \begin{enumerate}
148 |         \item If $n=2k+1$, then $C_\bullet(Gr(V))$ is exact. That is $H_t(Gr(V))=0$ for all $t$.
149 | 
150 |         \item If $n=2k$, then  $H_t(Gr(V))=0$ for all $t \neq k$.  That is all homology groups are trivial \emph{except the middle one}.
151 |     \end{enumerate}
152 | \end{theorem}
153 | 
154 | 
155 | 
156 | The same conclusion is true for the cohomology groups, as $H_t(Gr(V)) \cong H^{n-t}(Gr(V))$.
157 | 
158 | In \cite{MNUKHIN}, Mnukhin and Siemons asked for a different, direct, proof (''It would be desirable to have a direct proof of this result'').
159 | A key ingredient in our proof is a new construction of topological cones for the Grassmannian. In particular, \cite{MNUKHIN} conjectured that $Ker(\partial^{(R)})$ is generated by rather compact elements, for all rings $R$ for which $char(R)$ is coprime to $q$, (they named this conjecture by ''Projective Support Dimension Conjecture''). Our construction confirms their conjecture:

164 |    \emph{ $Ker(\partial^{(R)}_k)$ is generated by chains $\alpha_1,\ldots, \alpha_t$ such that each $\alpha_i$ is supported on a $2k$ dimensional subspace.
165 | }
166 | \vspace*{0.1px}

168 | The techniques we develop are robust and suitable for studying the homology of other posets (in particular differential posets), as well as proving $Ker(\partial_k)$ has compact generators.

173 | \subsection{Coboundary expansion}
174 | 
175 | Coboundary expansion was first defined in the foundational works of Linial, Meshulam and Wallach (\cite{LM}, \cite{MW}) on the $k$-homological connectivity of random complexes. It was later defined again in the pioneering work of Gromov on topological overlap \cite{Gromov}.
176 | 
177 | For a cochain $\alpha$ its size is defined by $|\alpha|=|\spp(\alpha)|$. Define the size of the cohomology class of $\alpha$ to be the minimum size over elements in the class of $\alpha$, that is
178 | \[\norm{[\alpha]} = \min\{ |\alpha - d_{k-1}\beta| \text{ for } \beta \in C^{k-1}\}.\]
179 | 
180 | \noindent With those two notions, one can generalize the coboundary expansion of graphs to other cochain complexes. The coboundary expansion at dimension $k$, $h_k$ is defined by:
181 | 
182 | \[h_k = \min_{\alpha \in C^k(X) \setminus B^{k}(X)} \left\{ \frac{|d_k\alpha|}{\norm{[\alpha]}} \right\} \]
183 | 
184 | In \Cref{sec : expansion} we show (with explicit bound on $C_k$):
185 | 
186 | 
187 | \vspace*{10px}
188 | \noindent\textbf{{Theorem  \ref{thm : expansion}.}}
189 |    \emph{ Let $h_{k,n}$ be the expansion constant of $k$ cochains of $Gr(V)$ where dim $V=n>2k$. There exist a constant $C_k$ that depends on $k,q$ such that:
190 |     \[h_{k,n} \geq  C_k {n \choose 1}_q\]}
191 | \vspace*{0.1px}
192 | 
193 | 
194 | We remark that our proof also applies in the case $q=1$, though the bound on the expansion constant is noticeably worse than the optimal one obtained by Meshulam and Wallach \cite{MW}.

196 | \subsection{Linial-Meshulam model for posets}
197 | 
198 | We consider the connectivity of Linial-Meshulam random $q$-complexes. Let $X \subseteq Gr(\mathbb{F}_q^n)$ be a $k+1$ dimensional complex with $X(k)=Gr_{k}(\mathbb{F}_q^n)$, the Linial-Meshulam random $q$-complex is the probability measure $\triangle^q(n,k,p)$ such that the probability of $X$ is:
199 | \[\mathbb{P}[X]=p^{|X(k+1)|}\cdot(1-p)^{ {n \choose k+1}_q - |X(k+1)|}\]
200 | 
201 | A $q$-complex is $k$ connected if $H^k(X)=0$. We show that Linial-Meshulam random $q$ complexes exhibit phase transition at $p = \ln  {n \choose k}_q  / {n-k \choose 1}_q $.
202 | 
203 | 
204 | \begin{theorem}\label{thm : qLMW}
205 |     Let $X \sim \triangle^q(n,k,p)$ be a random $k$ dimensional $q$-complex. For any $\omega(n) \rightarrow \infty$:
206 | 
207 |     \begin{enumerate}
208 |         \item If $p= \left( \ln \left( {n \choose k}_q \right) - \omega(n) \right)/ {n-k \choose 1}_q $ then $Pr[H^k(X) = 0] \rightarrow 0$.
209 | 
210 |         \item If $p=\left( \ln \left( {n \choose k}_q \right) + \omega(n) \right)/ {n-k \choose 1}_q $ then $Pr[H^k(X) = 0] \rightarrow 1$.
211 |     \end{enumerate}
212 | \end{theorem}

214 | \subsection{General definitions for graded posets}
215 | 
216 |  Consider $(X, \leq,rk )$ a ranked poset. That is, $(X,\leq)$ is a poset with a rank function $rk:X\to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ which satisfies (i) there is a unique element $0 \in X$ such that $rk(0)=0$, (ii) for $x < y$ one has $rk(x) < rk(y)$, and if there is no $z \in X$ such that $x < z < y$ then $rk(x)+1=rk(y)$. In such case one gets a natural partition of $X$ by $rk$ valuations $X=X(0) \cup X(1) \cup \ldots$, where $X(i)=\{x \in X \mid rk(x)=i\}$.
217 | 
218 | Define the space of chains $C_k(X)$, over $R$ to be the set of formal $R$ linear combinations of finitely many elements of $X(k)$, and the space of cochains as $C^k(X) = Hom(C_k(X), R)$. To have a notion of homology one needs a boundary signing map:
219 | 
220 | \[  [-,-] : X(k) \times X(k-1) \rightarrow R\]
221 | 
222 | If the boundary signing map is the constant $1$ function we say the boundary map is the modular boundary map. The boundary operator is defined by extending linearly:
223 | 
224 | \[ \partial (x) = \sum_{y \leq x , rk(y)=rk(x)-1} [x,y] y \]
225 | 
226 | This gives rise to the space of $k$-cycles $Z_k(X,R)=Ker(\partial_k)$, the space of $k$-boundaries $B_k(X,R)=Im(\partial_k)$, and when $\partial^2=0$ one can define the $k^{th}$ homology group of $X$ with $R$-coefficients by $H_k(X,R)=\faktor{Z_k(X,R)}{B_k(X,R)}$.
227 | 
228 | The space of cochains is defined naturally as $C^k(X) = Hom(C_k(X), R)$, and the coboundary operator is defined as being the adjoint of $\partial$, that is: $d_k \alpha (x) = \alpha(\partial_{k+1} x)$. The space of $k$-cocycles is $Z^k(X,R)=Ker(d_k)$, $k$-coboundaries $B^k(X,R)=Im(d_{k-1})$, and when $\partial^2=0$ one can define the $k^{th}$ cohomology of $X$ by $H^k(X,R)=\faktor{Z^k(X,R)}{B^{k-1}(X,R)}$.
229 | 
230 | For a cochain $\alpha$ define $|\alpha|=|\spp(\alpha)|$, the weight of it's cohomology class is \[\norm{[\alpha]} = \min\{ |\alpha - d_{k-1}\beta| \text{ for } \beta \in C^{k-1}\}.\]
231 | \noindent The \emph{$k^{th}$ cosystolic expansion constant} of $X$ is defined by:
232 | 
233 | \[h_k = \min_{\alpha \in C^k(X) \setminus Z^{k}(X)} \left\{ \frac{|d_k\alpha|}{\norm{[\alpha]}}  \right\} \]
234 | \begin{remark}
235 | In applications posets may also be associated with a weight function, see e.g. \cite{KTGarland}. Such a weight function indces a norm and inner product on the spaces of chains and cochains, which generalize the uniform weight we have used above. One can similarly define expansions with respect to such weights.
236 | \end{remark}
237 |

240 | Finally, the \emph{Linial-Meshulam random $X-$subcomplex of rank $k$ with probability $p$}, denoted by $\triangle(X,k,p)$ is the probability measure such that the probability supported on subposets $X'$ of $X$ satisfying $\cup_{i=0}^{k-1} X(i) \subseteq X' \subseteq \cup_{i=0}^{k} X(i)$ such that the events $y \in X'$ are i.i.d $Bernulli(p)$ random variables.

246 | \section{Homological Properties of $C_\bullet(Gr(V))$}\label{sec : homological properties}
247 | 
248 | This section studies the homological properties of $C_\bullet(Gr(V))$ for some subspace $V$. We start with the following useful Heisenberg-like identity that relates linearly the operators $\partial_{k+1}d_k, d_{k-1}\partial_k, id$:
249 | 
250 | \begin{claim}\label{claim: clifford}
251 |     If $n$ is odd then $\partial_{k+1}d_k - d_{k-1}\partial_k = (-1)^{k} id$
252 | \end{claim}
253 | 
254 | \begin{proof}
255 |     The term $d_{k-1}\partial_k (U)$ corresponds to \enquote{walking} down to a subspace of $U$ and then going up to a subspace containing it. The symmetric term corresponds to walking up and then down. Therefore if we look at the $U$ coordinate of L.H.S. $[d_{k-1}\partial_k(U)]_U = \qbin{k}{1}{q}$ since this corresponds to walking to a codimension $1$ subspaces of $U$ and returning to $U$. Similarly $[\partial_{k+1}d_k(U)]_U =\qbin{n-k}{1}{q}$ since this is the number of $W$ with $U \subseteq_1 W \subseteq V$.
256 | 
257 |     Notice that $\qbin{t}{1}{q}$ satisfies the following modulo $q+1$:
258 | 
259 |     \begin{align*}
260 |         \qbin{t}{1}{q} = \sum_{i=0}^{t-1} q^i = \sum_{i=0}^{t-1} (-1)^i = \mathds{1}_{(t \mod 2)=1}
261 |     \end{align*}
262 | 
263 |     Since $char(R) \mid q+1$ we get $\qbin{t}{1}{q} = (t \mod 2)$ in the ring $R$ as well. Therefore by the assumption that $n$ is odd:
264 | 
265 |     \begin{align*}
266 |         [\partial_{k+1}d_k (U) - d_{k-1}\partial_k (U) ]_U= (n-k \mod 2) - (k \mod 2)  = (-1)^{k}
267 |     \end{align*}
268 | 
269 |     For $W \neq U$ the walk $U \rightarrow U\cap W \rightarrow W$ exists if and only if the walk $U \rightarrow U + W \rightarrow W$ exists and these are the only possible walks, therefore, we get: $[\partial_{k+1}d_k (U) - d_{k-1}\partial_k (U)]_W=0$
270 | 
271 |     This concludes that $\partial_{k+1}d_k - d_{k-1}\partial_k = (-1)^{k} id$ for odd $n$.
272 | \end{proof}

282 | \subsection{Constructing cones}
283 | 
284 | A cone $c$ is a family of linear homomorphisms from $C_k(Gr(V))$ to $C_{k+1}(Gr(V))$ (they depend on $k$ which will usually be clear from context and therefore omitted) that satisfies the identity:
285 | 
286 | \begin{align}\label{eq - cone identity}
287 |     \partial c(\alpha) = \alpha - c(\partial \alpha)
288 | \end{align}
289 | 
290 | The name cone comes from the construction of a cone over a topological space that satisfies the identity above. We often have a few families of cones that depend on some index $b$, in this case, we will denote the cone with index $b$ of the chain $\alpha$ by $c_{b,\alpha}$.
291 | 
292 | In this subsection, we construct cones by using the \Cref{claim: clifford}. The main idea of our construction is to use the identity locally (while restricting it to some small subspace) instead of the whole space.
293 | 
294 | Let $\mathcal{B}$ be the set of all ordered bases of $V$. We will construct for each $(b_1,...,b_{n})= \vec{b} \in \mathcal{B}$ and $u \in C_k(X)$ a $k+1$ chain $c_{\vec{b},u}$ that satisfy $\partial c_{\vec{b},u} = u - c_{\vec{b},\partial(u)}$. 295 | \left(W_{\vec{b}}^{(i^*)} \right)= 2k+1$, define $W_{\vec{b}}:= W_{\vec{b}}^{(i^*)} $.
296 | 
297 | \begin{lemma}\label{lem : defin}
298 |     Let $\vec{b}$ be a basis of $V$, and let $W \leq V$ be a subspace of  $V$ of dimension $k$. Then:
299 | 
300 |     \begin{enumerate}
301 |         \item $W \leq W_{\vec{b}}$
302 | 
303 |         \item If $ W' \leq W$ then $W'_{\vec{b}}\leq W_{\vec{b}}$
304 |     \end{enumerate}
305 | \end{lemma}
306 | 
307 | \begin{proof}
308 | The first bullet is clear by definition of $W_{\vec{b}}$ as the first subspace of dimension $2k+1$ containing $W$ by appending vectors from $\vec{b}$.

337 | \begin{proposition}\label{Proposition - Cone Identity}
338 |     For $u\in C_k (Gr(V))$ we have $\partial c_{\vec{b},u} =  u - c_{\vec{b},\partial(u)}$
339 | \end{proposition}
340 | 
341 | \begin{proof}
342 |     It is enough to prove the identity for a $k$ space $W$. The proof is by induction on $k$. Indeed, by definition, we have:
343 | 
344 |     \begin{align*}
345 |         \partial (c_{\vec{b},W})  = \partial \left(  \left.d_k\right|_{W_{\vec{b}}} (-1)^{k} \left(W - c_{\vec{b},\partial(W)}  \right) \right)
346 |     \end{align*}
347 | 
348 | Since $dim (W_{\vec{b}})$ is odd we can apply \Cref{claim: clifford} and get that:
349 | 
350 |     \begin{align*}
351 |         \partial \left(  \left.d_k\right|_{W_{\vec{b}}}  (-1)^{k} \left(W - c_{\vec{b},\partial(W)}  \right)\right) &=   \left.d_{k-1}\right|_{W_{\vec{b}}} \partial_k  \left(  (-1)^{k} \left(W - c_{\vec{b},\partial(W)}  \right) \right) + (-1)^k \left( (-1)^{k} \left(W - c_{\vec{b},\partial(W)}  \right) \right) \\
352 |         &= \left.d_{k-1}\right|_{W_{\vec{b}}} \partial_k  \left(  (-1)^{k} \left(W - c_{\vec{b},\partial(W)}  \right) \right) +  \left(W - c_{\vec{b},\partial(W)}  \right)
353 |     \end{align*}
354 | 
355 |     To prove the claim it's enough to show that
356 |     \begin{align*}
357 |         \left.d_{k-1}\right|_{W_{\vec{b}}} \partial_k  \left(  (-1)^{k} \left(W - c_{\vec{b},\partial(W)}  \right) \right) =0
358 |     \end{align*}

364 |     &= (-1)^{k} \partial (W) + (-1)^{k+1} \partial (W)  = 0
365 | \end{align*}
366 | 
367 | \end{proof}

384 | We also note that in the space $Gr(\mathbb{F}_q^n)$ there is a projective duality between $k$ spaces and $n-k$ spaces that lifts to a duality between $k$-chains and $n-k$-cochains. Concretely, fix any non-degenerate bilinear form $\langle -,- \rangle$ on $\mathbb{F}_q^{n}$, e.g.  $\langle x,y \rangle =  \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \cdot  y_i$ and for a subspace $U$ define $U^{\perp} = \{v \in \mathbb{F}_q^{n} \mid \forall u \in U, \langle v,u \rangle = 0 \}$. 453 | \subsection{Expansion}\label{sec : expansion}
454 | 
455 | We now use the cones constructed for $Gr(V)$ to show that $Gr(V)$ has good coboundary expansion. Using cones to prove expansion is a technique developed in the work of Gromov \cite{Gromov} on topological overlap, and was implicitly defined in the work of Meshulam and Wallach \cite{MW}. Since then this technique was further developed and applied in greater generality e.g. \cite{LMM}, \cite{KOStrongSymmetry}.
456 | 
457 | We use this technique together with the cone construction of \Cref{sec : homological properties} to prove coboundary expansion of $Gr(V)$.
458 | 
459 | We remark that there is small set expansion, meaning that if a chain has a few subspaces then it must expand by simple counting:
460 | 
461 | \begin{claim}\label{claim: small-set-expansion}
462 |     For $\alpha \in C^k(X)$ we have $|d_k \alpha| \geq {n-k \choose 1}_q |\alpha| - |\alpha| \left(  |\alpha|-1 \right)$
463 | \end{claim}
464 | 
465 | \begin{proof}
466 |     Notice that every subspace in $\spp(\alpha)$ is contained in ${n-k \choose 1}_q$ subspaces of codimension $1$, so the number of subspaces that contain elements from $\alpha$ is at least:
467 | 
468 |     \begin{align*}
469 |         {n-k \choose 1}_q |\alpha| - {|\alpha| \choose 2}
470 |     \end{align*}
471 | 
472 |     Since out of the ${n-k \choose 1}_q |\alpha|$ subspaces we counted, the subspaces that contain $k$ elements from $\alpha$ are counted $k$ times and therefore by subtracting ${|\alpha| \choose 2}$ each such term is counted $ k- {k \choose 2} \leq 1$ times. There is clearly a surjection from ${\spp(\alpha) \choose 2}$ to the subspaces that contain more then one element, therefore:
473 | 
474 |     \begin{align*}
475 |         |d_k \alpha| \geq {n-k \choose 1}_q |\alpha| - {|\alpha| \choose 2} - {|\alpha| \choose 2} \geq {n-k \choose 1}_q |\alpha| - |\alpha| \left(  |\alpha|-1 \right)
476 |     \end{align*}
477 | \end{proof}

481 | \subsubsection{Coboundary expansion}
482 | 
483 | We now turn to prove high dimensional expansion of $Gr(V)$. We refer the intrested reader to \Cref{sec : exp by restrictions} for a different inductive argument for expansion of $1$-cochains via a different method of studying the expansion of a cochain by restricting it to a uniformly chosen, codimension $1$ hyperplane. We adapt the language of \cite{LMM}. We start with the following claim:
484 | 
485 | 
486 | \begin{claim}
487 |     $\iota_s d_k \alpha + d_{k-1}\iota_s \alpha =  \alpha$ where the contraction $\iota_s$ is given by $\iota_s \alpha (W) = \alpha(c_{s,W})$
488 | \end{claim}
489 | 
490 | \begin{proof}
491 |     Let $W$ be a subspace, then:
492 | 
493 |     \begin{align*}
494 |         \iota_s d_k \alpha (W) + d_{k-1}\iota_s \alpha(W) &= d_k \alpha (c_{s,W})+ \iota_s \alpha (\partial W) \\
495 |         &= \alpha (\partial c_{s,W}) + \alpha (c_{s,\partial W}) \\
496 |         &= \alpha (\partial c_{s,W} + c_{s,\partial W})
497 |     \end{align*}
498 |     By \Cref{Proposition - Cone Identity} we know this equals to $\alpha(W)$ as required.
499 | \end{proof}
500 | 
501 | \begin{claim}\label{claim : cone bound}
502 |     Let $\vec{b}$ be any basis and $W$ any $k$ space then: \begin{align*}
503 |         |c_{\vec{b},W}| \leq [k+1]_q \left( [k]_q!^2 \cdot \left( 1 +  \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{1}{[j]_q!^2} \right)\right)
504 |     \end{align*}
505 | \end{claim}
506 | 
507 | \begin{proof}
508 | 
509 |     Let $f(k)$ be a bound of the size of a cone of a $k$ space. By definition, $c_{\vec{b},W}$ is the coboundary of a $(-1)^k \left(W- c_{\vec{b}, \partial W} \right)$ in some $2k+1$ therefore:
510 | 
511 |     \begin{align*}
512 |         |c_{\vec{b},W}| \leq [k+1]_q \left(|W|+| c_{\vec{b}, \partial W}|\right) \leq [k+1]_q \left( 1 + [k]_q f(k-1) \right)
513 |     \end{align*}
514 | 
515 |     Thus,
516 |     \begin{align}\label{eq : recursive cone bound}
517 |         f(k) \leq [k+1]_q \left( 1 + [k]_q f(k-1) \right)
518 |     \end{align}
519 |     The proof follows since the RHS of the equation in the claim satisfies \Cref{eq : recursive cone bound}, and bounds the cone size for zero dimensional subspace.
520 | \end{proof}
521 | 
522 | \begin{remark}
523 |     This bound is stronger than the bound $|c_{\vec{b},W}| \leq  { 2k+1 \choose k+1}_q $ by roughly a factor of $q^k$.
524 | \end{remark}
525 | 
526 | \begin{theorem}\label{thm : expansion}
527 | 
528 |     Let $h_{k,n}$ be the expansion constant of $k$ cochains of $Gr(V)$ where dim $V=n$. Let $b$ be the bound of the cone size for $k$ chains, and assume $k< \frac{n}{2}$. Then:
529 | 
530 |     \begin{enumerate}
531 |         \item $h_{k,n} \geq  \frac{[n-k]_q}{[k+1]_q} \cdot \frac{1}{b}$, where $b$ bounds the cone size.
532 | 
533 |         \item $h_{n-k,n} \geq \frac{1}{b}$.
534 |     \end{enumerate}
535 | 
536 | \end{theorem}
537 | 
538 | \begin{proof}
539 | 
540 |     For the first item, let $S$ be the set of all bases of $\mathbb{F}_q^n$ then
541 | 
542 |     \begin{align*}
543 |         |S| \cdot  \norm{ [\alpha] } &\leq \sum_{s \in S} \norm{\alpha -  d_{k-1}\iota_s \alpha }  \\
544 |         &= \sum_{s \in S} \norm{ \iota_s d_k \alpha } \\
545 |         &\leq \sum_{s \in S} \sum_{W \in Gr(k,n)} \mathds{1}_{d_k \alpha (c_{s,W})\neq 0} \\
546 |         &\leq \sum_{s \in S} \sum_{W \in Gr(k,n)} \mathds{1}_{\spp(c_{s,W}) \cap \spp( d_k \alpha) \neq \emptyset}
547 |     \end{align*}
548 | 
549 |     By symmetry each element of $Gr(k+1,n)$ will be in the support of $c_{s,W}$ the same number of times over all $s \in S$ and $W \in Gr(k,n)$. This is true in particular for all the elements of $\spp( d_k \alpha)$ therefore we can bound the last term above by $|S|\cdot |Gr(k,n)| \cdot b \cdot \norm{d_k \alpha}$ thus:
550 | 
551 |     \begin{align*}
552 |         |S| \cdot  \norm{ [\alpha] } \leq \frac{|S|\cdot |Gr_{k}(n)| \cdot b}{|Gr_{k+1}(n)|} \cdot \norm{d_k \alpha}
553 |     \end{align*}
554 | 
555 |     Which implies:
556 | 
557 |     \begin{align*}
558 |         \frac{\norm{d_k \alpha}}{\norm{ [\alpha] }}   \geq \frac{ |Gr_{k+1}(n)| }{|Gr_{k}(n)| \cdot  b} = \frac{[n-k]_q}{[k+1]_q} \cdot \frac{1}{b}
559 |     \end{align*}
560 | 
561 |     For the second item, each closed chain $\alpha \in Z_k$ can be filled by $c_{\vec{b},\alpha}$ for some basis $\alpha$ which has size at most $b |\alpha|$. The claim follows by \Cref{Claim - Bilinear form duality}.
562 | \end{proof}

583 | It is clear by the observation that $\eta_i \in Z_i(Gr(2i))$. One can also write an explicit formula for $\eta_n$. For any sequence of $(\epsilon_i)_{i=1}^n \in \{0,1\}^{n}$ and $A=\{a_{i,j}\}_{ 1 \leq i \leq j < n}$ with $a_{i,j} \in \mathbb{F}_q$ there will be a vector space in the support of $\eta_n$. Explicitly, define the following vector:
584 | \begin{align*}
585 |     v^{(\epsilon, A)}_i:= \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} a_{i,j} e_{2j-\epsilon_j} + e_{2i-1} + \epsilon_{i} \left( e_{2i}-e_{2i-1} \right)
586 | \end{align*}

591 |     W^{(\epsilon,A)} := Span \{ v^{(\epsilon, A)}_1, \ldots, v^{(\epsilon, A)}_n \}
592 | \end{align*}

595 | 
596 | \begin{claim}[James \cite{JamesRepTheory}]\label{claim explicit form of two cones}
597 |     $\eta_n = (-1)^{ { n \choose 2}} \sum_{\epsilon \in \{0,1\}^{n},A} sgn(\epsilon) W^{(\epsilon,A)}$ , where the sum is over $A$ as above.
598 | \end{claim}

600 | \begin{proof}
601 |     The proof is by induction. For $n=1$ we have $c_{\vec{b}^{+}_{2}, \eta_{0}}=c_{e_1,0}= span\{e_1\}$, and similarily $c_{\vec{b}^{-}_{2}, \eta_{0}}= Span\{e_2\}$ thus by definition $\eta_1= Span\{e_1\} - Span\{e_2\}$.

We can decompose $c_{\vec{b}^{+}_{2i}, \eta_{n}} = \beta^+ + \gamma^+$ where $\beta^+$ is supported on subspaces contained in $Span \{ e_1,\ldots, e_{2n} \}$ and $\gamma^+$ support is not contained in $Span \{ e_1,\ldots, e_{2n} \}$.

605 |     We can decompose analogously $c_{\vec{b}^{-}_{2i}, \eta_{n}} = \beta^- + \gamma^-$. We know that $\beta^+=\beta^- = \restr{d \eta_{n}}{Span\{e_1, \ldots, e_{2n}\}}$. We conclude that:

621 | \subsubsection{Construction of $\psi_n$}
622 | 
623 | We now consider a different $q$-analog of the cross polytope, denoted by $\psi_n$. The construction is based on the theory of polar spaces and specifically, the polar space associated with the orthogonal quadratic form. We will review the facts we need from the theory of quadratic forms over a finite field to construct $\psi_n$ (we refer the reader to  \cite{Aschbacher}, \cite{Chevalley} for a thorough treatment)
624 | 
625 | Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{F}_q$. Let $Q: V \rightarrow \mathbb{F}_q $ be a quadratic form, that is $Q$ satisfies $Q(av)=a^2Q(v)$ and also $B(u,v)=Q(u+v)-Q(u)-Q(v)$ is a billinear form. We assume $B$ is non-degenerate. In the cases $char(\mathbb{F}_q)\neq 2$ one can work with a symmetric bilinear form directly, but for $char(\mathbb{F}_q) = 2$ the extra information of $Q$ is necessary. A vector $v$ is \emph{singular} if $Q(v)=0$. A pair of singular vectors $\{e,f\}$ is a \emph{hyperbolic pair}, if $B(e,f)=1$.
626 | 
627 | \begin{fact}\label{decomposition to hyperbolic pairs}
628 |     Every $V$ of dimension $2n$ as above has a decomposition $V=Span \{e_1,f_1, \ldots ,e_n,f_n \} $ where:
629 | 
630 |     \begin{enumerate}

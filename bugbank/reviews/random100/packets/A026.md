33 | We study the fundamental scheduling problem $1\|\sum p_jU_j$. Given a set of $n$ jobs with processing times $p_j$ and deadlines $d_j$, the problem is to select a subset of jobs such that the total processing time is maximized without violating the deadlines. In the midst of a flourishing line of research, Fischer and Wennmann have recently devised the sought-after $\widetilde O(P)$-time algorithm, where $P = \sum p_j$ is the total processing time of all jobs. This running time is optimal as it matches conditional lower bounds based on popular conjectures.

42 | \section{Introduction}
43 | 
44 | The $1 \| \sum p_jU_j$ problem is a classical NP-hard problem that generalizes the well-known Subset Sum problem. Given $n$ jobs with processing times $p_j$ and deadlines $d_j$, the problem is to select a subset of jobs such that the total processing time is maximized under the constraint that their deadlines are observed. Its history goes back to the 55-year old algorithm by Lawler and Moore~\cite{lawler_moore}, whose running time reads $O(n P)$, where $P = \sum p_j$.
45 | 
46 | \sparagraph{Multiple Machines.} The problem also appears in the multiple-machine flavor as the so-called $P_m\|\sum p_jU_j$ problem: In this setting, there are $m$ machines among which the jobs can be distributed; the task remains the same as before. For simplicity, we assume that $m$ is a constant.
47 | 
48 | \sparagraph{Related Work.} Interestingly, the running time of Lawler and Mooore's algorithm remained the state-of-the-art for quite some time: Only recently did Bringmann, Fischer, Hermelin, Shabtay and Wellnitz~\cite{ref_12} manage to lower it to $\widetilde O(P^{7/4})$ by introducing, as a by-product, a novel convolution -- the \emph{skewed convolution}.\footnote{The term $\widetilde O(P) = P(\log P)^{O(1)}$ suppresses polylogarithmic factors.} Following their work, a line of research started to improve either the algorithmic reduction or the skewed convolution itself, resulting in a $\widetilde O(P^{7/5})$-time algorithm~\cite{ref_28, ref_36}. Recently, Fischer and Wennmann~\cite{baseline} showed that the skewed convolution can be forgotten altogether: Indeed, they provide a randomized algorithm in time $O(P \log P)$ and, alternatively, a deterministic one in time $O(P \log^{1+o(1)} P)$. In particular, their algorithm is optimal (up to lower-order factors) under the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis or the Set Cover Hypothesis.
49 | 
50 | Similar to the Subset Sum problem, our problem also has other parameters that can be used to parametrize the running time by. Prominent examples are $\dmax = \max d_j$ and $\pmax = \max p_j$. Prior research also introduced algorithm w.r.t.~to these parameters: an $\widetilde O(\min\{P\cdot D_{\#}, P + D\})$-time algorithm~\cite{ref_12}, where $\#D$ is the number of distinct deadlines and $D$ is the sum of all distinct deadlines, and an $\widetilde O(n + \pmax^3)$-time algorithm~\cite{ref_28}. Fischer and Wennmann explicitly leave open the question of whether a time of $\widetilde O(n + \dmax)$ or even $\widetilde O(n + \pmax)$ is possible~\cite{baseline}.
51 | 
52 | \sparagraph{Our Results.} We show that an $\widetilde O(n + \dmax)$-time algorithm does indeed exist. Perhaps surprisingly, our algorithm is a simple refined implementation of Fischer and Wennmann's. Our analysis also extends to the multi-machine setting, resulting in a $\widetilde O(n + \dmax^m)$-time algorithm.

54 | \section{Preliminaries}
55 | 
56 | \sparagraph{Notation.} We use $[n] \vcentcolon=\{1, \ldots, n\}$ (note that Fischer and Wennmann's notation is 0-based~\cite{baseline}). The interval $[i..j]$ denotes $\{i, \ldots, j\}$, while $[i..j)$ denotes $[i, j] \setminus \{j\}$; analogously, define $(i..j]$. Furthermore, we use the standard sumset notation $A + b = \{a + b \mid a \in A\}$ and $A + B = \{a + b \mid a \in A, b \in B\}$.
57 | 
58 | \sparagraph{Earliest-Deadline-First.} We employ the key observation due to Lawler and Moore~\cite{lawler_moore} that w.l.o.g.~the \emph{early}, i.e., non-tardy, jobs may be scheduled in the sorted order of their deadlines. Thus, the problem reduces to computing a subset of jobs $J^* \subseteq [n]$ that maximizes $\sum_{j\in J^*} p_j$ and observes the deadlines of the jobs within $J^*$, i.e., $\sum_{i\in J^*:i \leq j} p_i \leq d_j$ for all $j \in J$.
59 | 
60 | \sparagraph{Lawler and Moore's Algorithm.} The algorithm due to Lawler and Moore is a simple dynamic program. Namely, one recursively defines the sets $S_0, \ldots, S_n \subseteq [0..P]$ as in the following:
61 | \begin{algorithm}[H]
62 | \label{alg:original_alg}
63 | \caption{Lawler and Moore's algorithm}
64 | \begin{subequations}
65 | \begin{align}
66 |     S_0 &= \{0\},\label{eq:1st}\\
67 |     S_{j}' &= S_{j-1} + \{0, p_j\},\label{eq:2nd}\\
68 |     S_{j} &= S_{j}' \cap [0..d_j],\label{eq:3rd}
69 | \end{align}
70 | \end{subequations}
71 | where $j \in [n]$.
72 | \end{algorithm}
73 | Note that we follow the two-step construction of $S_{j}$ proposed by Fischer and Wennmann~\cite{baseline}, since it allows for a modular implementation. The optimal value can be then read off as $\max S_n$.
74 | 
75 | \sparagraph{Fischer and Wennmann's Algorithm.} The algorithm proposed by Fischer and Wennmann~\cite{baseline} uses the dynamic string data structure~\cite{ref_21, ref_27}, recently employed in modular subset sum~\cite{ref_4, ref_14, ref_34}. Their key observation is that one can use the same data structure to support the deletions in Eq.~\eqref{eq:3rd}. In particular, one has to interpret the sets $S_{j}$ as indicator strings of size $P$.
76 | 
77 | \sparagraph{Our Approach.} However, observe that in Eq.~\eqref{eq:3rd}, we anyway \emph{trim} the current set $S_{j} + \{0, p_j\}$ by its processing times greater than $d_j$. Hence, if we had a mechanism to only add \emph{valid} processing times, i.e., l.e.q.~$d_j$, we could interpret the sets $S_{j}$ indeed as indicator strings of size $\dmax$ + 1.\footnote{The offset of ``+1'' is necessary to account for deadlines of value 0.}
78 | 
79 | \section{A Refined Algorithm}
80 | 
81 | First, observe that, by construction, the following invariant holds: $S_{j-1} \subseteq [0..d_{j-1}]$. The new algorithm works as follows. Instead of directly performing Eq.~\eqref{eq:2nd} as is, we first split $S_j$ into two parts w.r.t.~to $d_j - p_j$, i.e., we obtain $L_{j-1} \vcentcolon= S_{j-1} \cap [0..d_j - p_j]$ and $R_{j-1} \vcentcolon= S_{j-1} \cap (d_j - p_j..d_j]$. We will see later how $L_{j-1}$ and $R_{j-1}$ can be computed via the dynamic string data structure. We then compute $\widetilde S_j = L_{j-1} + p_j$. Finally, we need to merge $\widetilde S_j$ with both $L_{j-1}$ and $R_{j-1}$ to obtain the final $S_j$. Better said, we are left with merging $\widetilde S_j$ with $S_{j-1}$ to get $S_j = \widetilde S_j \cup S_{j-1}$.
82 | \begin{algorithm}[H]
83 | \caption{Refined algorithm}
84 | \begin{subequations}
85 | \begin{align}
86 |     S_0 &= \{0\},\label{eq:adapted_1st}\\
87 |     L_{j-1} &= S_{j-1} \cap [0..d_j - p_j],\label{eq:adapted_2nd_1}\\
88 | 
89 |     \widetilde S_{j} &= L_{j - 1} + p_j,\label{eq:adapted_3rd}\\
90 |     S_{j} &= \widetilde S_{j} \cup S_{j-1},\label{eq:adapted_4th}
91 | \end{align}
92 | \end{subequations}
93 | where $j \in [n]$.
94 | \end{algorithm}
95 | 
96 | In the following, we outline the implementation details.
97 | 
98 | \sparagraph{Leveraging Dynamic Strings.} The dynamic string data structure is an important toolbox in Fischer and Wennmann's algorithm~\cite{baseline}. In the sequel, we use their presentation of the data structure and outline only the operations we will need:
99 | \begin{lemma}[Dynamic String Data Structure~\cite{ref_21,ref_27}]
100 |     The dynamic string data structure maintains a dynamic collection $X$ of non-empty strings, supporting the following operations:
101 |     \begin{itemize}
102 |         \item \texttt{make\_string}$(x)$: Given any string $x \in \Sigma^+$, insert $x$ into $X$.
103 |         \item \texttt{concat}$(x_1, x_2)$: Given $x_1, x_2 \in X$, insert the concatenated string $x_1x_2$ into $X$.
104 |         \item \texttt{split}$(x, i)$: Given $x \in X$ and $i \in [0..|x|)$, insert $x[0..i]$ and $x(i..|x|]$ into $X$.
105 |     \end{itemize}
106 |     Let $s$ be the maximum of the total length of all strings and the number of executed operations. Then all operations run in randomized time $O(\log s)$ or in deterministic time $O(\log^{1 + o(1)}s)$, except for \texttt{make\_string} which takes time $O(|x| + \log s)$ and $O(|x| \cdot \log^{o(1)}s)$, respectively.
107 | \end{lemma}
108 | Let $u$ be the string size (this will be set to $\dmax + 1$ later). We represent a set $S \subseteq [n]$ by its corresponding indicator string $x_S \in \{0, 1\}^u$, i.e., $i \in S$ if and only if $x_S[i] = 1$.
109 | 
110 | To initialize the data structure, we build $0^{u}$ by inserting the word $w = 0$ and self-concatenating it $\log u$ times. This takes time $O(\log^2 u)$.
111 | 
112 | Then, to obtain, $L_{j - 1}$, we split $x_{S_{j-1}}$ at position $d_j - p_j$. Afterward, we can compute $\widetilde S_j$ by shifting $x_{L_{j - 1}}$ by $p_j$ positions. Namely, we perform $\texttt{concat}(0^{p_j}, x_{L_{j-1}})$, where the string $0^{p_j}$ can be obtained by splitting off the above pre-computed $0^{u}$ via $\texttt{split}(0^u, p_j)$. We finally trim the resulting string to length $u$ via $\texttt{split}(0^{p_j}x_{L_{j-1}}, u)$. Merging $\widetilde S_j$ with $S_{j - 1}$ is now a bit-wise OR of $x_{\widetilde S_j}$ and $x_{S_{j - 1}}$ and can be implemented in the same way as by Fischer and Wennmann~\cite{baseline}.
113 | 
114 | \sparagraph{Running Time Analysis.}
115 | Our running time analysis closely follows that by Fischer and Wennmann~\cite{baseline}, with the key difference that, using our new implementation, we manage to bound the number of insertions by $\dmax + 1$ instead of $2P + 1$:
116 | \begin{lemma}
117 |     \label{lemma:refined_single}
118 |     It holds that $\displaystyle\sum_{i\in [n]} |\widetilde S_{j} \setminus S_{j - 1}| \leq \dmax + 1$.
119 | \end{lemma}
120 | \begin{proof}
121 |     A benefit of the new algorithm is that, compared to Ref.~\cite{baseline}, we do not need to split w.r.t.~$d_j$ since the set $S_j$ is, \emph{by construction}, already filtered out of processing times greater than $d_j$. Hence, for any $x \in [0..\dmax]$, once $x \in \widetilde S_{j} \setminus S_{j-1}$, then $x \in S_{j}, \ldots, S_{n}$ and, hence, we have
122 |     \[
123 |     |\{i \in [n] \mid x \in \widetilde S_j \setminus S_{j - 1}\}| \leq 1.
124 |     \]
125 |     With this observation, the cumulative number of insertions reads
126 |     \[
127 |         \displaystyle\sum_{i\in[n]}|\widetilde S_{j} \setminus S_{j-1}| = \displaystyle\sum_{x \in [0.. \dmax]} |\{i \in [n] \mid x \in \widetilde S_j \setminus S_{j - 1}\}| \leq \dmax + 1.
128 |     \]
129 | \end{proof}
130 | Using the dynamic string data structure with string-size $u = \dmax + 1$, we obtain an $\widetilde O(n + \dmax)$-time algorithm.
131 | 
132 | \sparagraph{Retrieving the Schedule.} We have shown that we can compute the optimal value in time $\widetilde O(n + \dmax)$ by a refined implementation of the original algorithm. Obtaining the optimal schedule can be, indeed, done in a similar way, since the sets themselves are after each iteration the same as in Fischer and Wennmann's algorithm. We skip the details and refer the reader to Ref.~\cite[Sec.~3.2]{baseline}.
133 | 
134 | \section{Generalizing to Multiple Machines}
135 | 
136 | Our refined analysis can be extended to the setting of $m$ machines. In that context, the original dynamic program slightly changes, but the core idea remains the same. In particular, we can define similar sets $S_0, \ldots, S_n$ which are this time subsets of $[0..P]^{m}$, i.e., the $i$-th entry of each vector corresponds to the $i$-th machine:
137 | \begin{algorithm}[H]
138 | \label{alg:original_alg}
139 | \caption{Lawler and Moore's Algorithm for $m$ machines}
140 | \begin{subequations}
141 | \begin{align}
142 |     S_0 &= \{0\},\label{eq:1st}\\
143 |     S_{j}' &= S_{j-1} + \{0, p_j \cdot e_1, \ldots, p_j \cdot e_m\},\label{eq:2nd}\\
144 |     S_{j} &= S_{j}' \cap [0..d_j]^m,\label{eq:3rd}
145 | \end{align}
146 | \end{subequations}
147 | for each $j \in [n]$ and $e_1, \ldots, e_m$ are the standard unit vectors in $\mathbb{Z}^m$.
148 | \end{algorithm}
149 | A feasible schedule of total processing time $t$ exists if and only if there exists $s \in S_n$ such that $s_1 + \ldots + s_m = t$~\cite{lawler_moore}. It is now easy to see that we can apply our refined algorithm for the single-machine setting to this setting as well. The key idea is to apply our trimming strategy point-wise, for each of the $m$ (independent) entries of the vectors.
150 | \begin{algorithm}[H]
151 | \caption{Refined algorithm for $m$ machines}
152 | \begin{subequations}
153 | \begin{align}
154 |     S_0 &= \{0\},\label{eq:1st}\\
155 |     L_{j - 1} &= S_{j - 1} \cap [0..d_j - p_j]^m,\label{eq:adapted_2nd_1}\\
156 |     \widetilde S_j &= L_{j-1} + \{0, p_j \cdot e_1, \ldots, p_j \cdot e_m\},\label{eq:adapted_3rd}\\
157 |     S_{j} &= \widetilde S_{j} \cup S_{j-1},\label{eq:adapted_4th}
158 | \end{align}
159 | \end{subequations}
160 | where $j \in [n]$.
161 | \end{algorithm}
162 | We can similarly bound the number of insertions:
163 | \begin{lemma}
164 | It holds that $\sum_{j\in[n]}|\widetilde S_j \setminus S_{j-1}| \leq (\dmax + 1)^m$.
165 | \end{lemma}
166 | \begin{proof}
167 |     We closely follow our proof for Lemma~\ref{lemma:refined_single}. Namely, we make the observation that for any $x \in [0..\dmax]^m$, once $x \in \widetilde S_j \setminus S_{j - 1}$, then $x \in S_j, \ldots, S_n$. Hence, we have
168 |     \[
169 |     |\{j \in [n] \mid x \in \widetilde S_j \setminus S_{j - 1}\}| \leq 1.
170 |     \]
171 |     Therefore, we can bound the total number of insertions by
172 |     \[
173 |     \displaystyle\sum_{j\in[n]}|\widetilde S_j \setminus S_{j-1}| = \displaystyle\sum_{x\in [0..\dmax]^m} |\{j \in [n] \mid x \in \widetilde S_j \setminus S_{j - 1}\}| \leq (\dmax + 1)^m.
174 |     \]
175 | \end{proof}
176 | The rest of the algorithm, in particular the adapted multi-dimensional dynamic string data structure, remains the same as in Fischer and Wennmann~\cite{baseline}.
177 | 
178 | \section{Open Problems}
179 | 
180 | We settled an open question explicitly asked by Fischer and Wennmann~\cite{baseline}. Somewhat surprisingly, we obtained it by slightly modifying their original algorithm, resulting in a $\widetilde O(n + \dmax)$-time algorithm. A pressing open question is whether the problem can be solved in $\widetilde O(n + \pmax)$-time. Indeed, it would be (again) surprising if the same algorithm could be adapted to this setting. However, such an algorithm would have a wider implication, since it would also represent a break-through for Subset Sum with small items. In spite of that, the recent break-through by Chen, Lian, Mao and Zhang~\cite{subset_sum_break}, namely an $\widetilde O(n + \sqrt{w t})$-time algorithm for the Subset Sum problem, shows that a similar running time for the $1 \| \sum p_j U_j$ problem may not be completely out of reach.
